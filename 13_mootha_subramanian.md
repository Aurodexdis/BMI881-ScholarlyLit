## *Gene Set Enrichment Articles* $-$ Mootha et al. 2003 & Subramanian et al. 2005

### Summary:
Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), a powerful analytical technique for understanding genome-wide expression data by looking at clusters of functionally related genes rather than individual genes in isolation, is introduced and validated in these fundamental studies. In their investigation of type 2 diabetes, Mootha et al. initially used an early version of GSEA. They found that diabetic muscle samples, especially those that respond to PGC-1α, exhibit minor but coordinated downregulation of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation. The fully developed GSEA methodology, including crucial improvements like weighted enrichment score and false discovery rate analysis, is then presented in the study by Subramanian et al.. It also illustrates the technology's wide range of biological applications, including cancer research. These studies collectively demonstrated that GSEA is a reliable method for identifying subtle yet biologically significant changes in gene expression that conventional single-gene analysis might overlook.

### Reaction:
The way these articles address the basic genomics problem—extracting significant biological insights from intricate expression datasets where significant changes may be minor at the individual gene level—is what was most interesting to me (even though I have already learned about this before in classes, it's cool to see original research papers regarding the issue.) Examining coordinated alterations across functionally related gene sets is the authors' simple and effective answer to this problem. I was pleased with how the Subramanian paper's modified GSEA method enhanced the original strategy while preserving its essential features, particularly managing gene-gene correlations and multiple hypothesis testing. Another important contribution to the discipline is the development of MSigDB, a meticulously managed library of gene sets. More consideration of potential drawbacks, such as the reliance on predetermined gene sets and presumptions regarding gene independence, would have been helpful, though.

### Discussion Questions:
1. To properly analyze single-cell RNA sequencing data, when technical noise and dropout effects provide additional analytical problems, how could the GSEA approach be adjusted or modified?
2. The authors show how useful GSEA is for evaluating separate research on lung cancer survival. What are the limitations of this approach and how might it be used to solve the more general problem of reproducibility in genomics research?
3. Both studies demonstrate how GSEA can detect minute yet well-coordinated alterations in gene expression linked to illness conditions. How might this methodology be expanded to incorporate more biological data types (such as proteomics and metabolomics) to offer a more thorough understanding of disease mechanisms?
