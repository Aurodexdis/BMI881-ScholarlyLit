## *Cluster failure: Why fMRI inferences for spatial extent have inflated false-positive rates* $-$ Eklund et al.

### Summary:
By analyzing resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data from 499 healthy controls, Eklund et al. conducted a thorough assessment of statistical techniques utilized in fMRI analysis tools (SPM, FSL, and AFNI). They examined the efficacy of parametric and non-parametric approaches for regulating familywise error rates in brain activation research using more than 3 million random group analyses. According to their study, popular parametric statistical techniques were problematic for clusterwise inference and conservative for voxelwise inference, with false-positive rates as high as 70% rather than the nominal 5%. Spatial autocorrelation functions that had heavier tails and departed from the expected Gaussian form were shown to be the main reason. This study raised serious questions regarding the validity of several published fMRI studies and sparked important discussions around statistical methods in neuroimaging.

### Reaction:
The innovative use of resting-state data as null data to test statistical methods caught my attention as it offered a practical framework for validation rather than depending on simulated data. I was quite surprised by the discovery of a 15-year-old bug in AFNI's 3dClustSim function, but the effect it had on false-positive rates seemed insignificant in comparison to the broader methodological problems detected. The commentaries demonstrated how the study generated constructive debate among the neuroimaging community, resulting in more complex interpretations of the results and highlighting the value of open science and data sharing. The existence of the spatial autocorrelation problem, even in phantom MRI data, indicates that this is a basic problem in fMRI analysis that needs to be carefully taken into account.

### Discussion Questions:
1. What methods might be used to reassess or validate previously published findings, and how can the results of this study affect how previous fMRI research is interpreted?
2. Given the proven effectiveness of non-parametric techniques in reducing false-positive rates, what are the real-world obstacles and possible solutions to increase the accessibility and widespread use of these computationally demanding techniques in fMRI research?
3. What effects does the identification of non-Gaussian spatial autocorrelation in task and resting-state fMRI data have on our knowledge of brain activity patterns and connectivity, and what does this mean for the design of future research?
